# 3. Rules [normative]

### Security

- Every security decision of the application lives in `rules.md`, and this section is their record. No security decision is taken without discussion, and none is decided outside here: the other sections describe operation and detail what is recorded below, but they do not decide security on their own. If it is not recorded here, it does not exist.

- Isolation belongs to the container path. On the hosted path AT is a process on the machine, with no container around it, and that is declared — there is no promise of containing the blast radius of a compromise. What the hosted path offers in exchange is listening only on the local loop by default, and demanding authentication and TLS to leave it.

- Decisions in force. The line is the decision; the detail lives where the link points:
  - AT answers on `127.0.0.1` by default, and that is what this product recommends; another address is an adjustment of the folder, and what it costs is what the environment's security note evaluates — [Access and exposure](#access-and-exposure).
  - Exposed demands TLS, and TLS only exists with Caddy: `at-cli webapp` without the flag serves HTTP, and who leaves the loop is the Caddy — [Access and exposure](#access-and-exposure).
  - AT has an authenticator of its own, with four ways in, all of them ending at an actor: local Forms, Basic, machine credential and the identity the gate asserts — [Interfaces](interfaces.md), [Tables](architecture.md#tables).
  - The actor's credential lives hashed in its own row, with `libsodium`; Basic and Forms are the **same** credential, and what separates them is the transport — [Tables](architecture.md#tables).
  - No secret lives in `settings.json`: it holds permission and adjustment, never a credential — [General settings](architecture.md#general-settings).
  - Every function of the core API is protected by RBAC; the permission is `object:action`, a role is a set of permissions, and a machine credential has a declared role — [Tables](architecture.md#tables).
  - **The first admin is born on the first start, in a PAGE and on the LOOP.** A folder with no administrator at all comes up, and the page it serves is the form that creates the first one — name and Main Key (16+, one phrase), the same strings `at-cli admin` uses (item 176). **"With no actor at all AT does not expose" still holds, and it is what the rule buys**: it does not come up off the loop — not through `--host`, not behind the Caddy —, and the route that writes without a session refuses the request that did not come over the loop. The population is the one the terminal already served: whoever is at the machine — [Interfaces](interfaces.md), [Access and exposure](#access-and-exposure).
  - **Running from source the WebApp asks nobody in**; the `dist`/release binary always asks, even in debug — and the key is the artifact, never a flag — [Access and exposure](#access-and-exposure).
  - Role and permission are a WebApp screen, and only an admin touches them — [Interfaces](interfaces.md).
  - **Installing a service is the Wizard's and only the Wizard's** — [Interfaces](interfaces.md).
  - A machine secret is stored hashed, with a role; a secret AT **uses**, such as a service credential, is stored encrypted in the `secret` table — [The Vault](architecture.md#the-vault).
  - A secret AT uses is **sealed with the key of its own service or purpose**: the key is derived from the Main Key and recovered from it alone, and what one scope sealed another does not open — [The Vault](architecture.md#the-vault).
  - AT authenticates to the services instead of taking advantage of being on the same machine: an administrative credential exists where it is necessary, and it counts in the posture — [The security posture](#the-security-posture).
  - A service that talks to AT gets a delimited scope — least privilege: the compromise of a service reaches its function and nothing else — [Container path](paths.md#container-path).
  - Identity arriving through the gate is only valid with the gate secret: AT draws a secret per installation, writes it into the Caddyfile it generates itself, and refuses identity without it — [Install the identity provider as a container](container.md#b-install-the-identity-provider-as-a-container-required).
  - The gate proves the credential the actor's row already carries: AT writes the identity provider the folder's own people, with the derivation that row holds, and both the names and the derivations stay inside the Working Folder, which is what the backup covers — [Install the identity provider as a container](container.md#b-install-the-identity-provider-as-a-container-required).
  - **Changing a person's credential is one act, over the row and over the gate**: the credential goes into the actor's row, the `users.yml` is derived from it, and the provider comes up again so that the new derivation is the one it proves. **The restart does not evict the secret, but it does evict the sessions already open**: the session secret is drawn once and kept, and it is what seals the cookie — but the registry of who is logged in is the provider's own MEMORY (§7b), and a recreated container zeroes it, so a credential changed by this act takes down every open session, not just the one it changed — [Install the identity provider as a container](container.md#b-install-the-identity-provider-as-a-container-required).
  - **Notification is the PWA's own Web Push, not a service of the environment.** Whoever fulfils the `notifier` (§4) is `at/core/push.py`, inside the `at-cli` that serves the WebApp: it draws the VAPID pair (P-256) in the folder, keeps the subscriptions the browsers send, and encrypts each alert with `aes128gcm` (RFC 8291) and the `ES256` JWT (RFC 8292) on delivery — [Notification](container.md#f-notification-the-pwas-web-push-no-container).
  - **The browser receives only the PUBLIC key, and never a secret.** The page asks for the public VAPID (`/api/push/key`) to subscribe and returns the subscription (`/api/push/subscribe`); the private half never leaves the folder, and the page never holds a notification credential — the route that reads the alerts ([`notifier:read`](architecture.md#tables)) answers the messages, never what opened them — [Notification](container.md#f-notification-the-pwas-web-push-no-container).
  - **The phone reaches no port beyond the site.** It opens the same `https://<name>`, installs the PWA and turns notifications on; from then on the one talking to it is the browser's OWN push service. The only port leaving the machine is still `443` — [Notification](container.md#f-notification-the-pwas-web-push-no-container).
  - **Delivery is best-effort**: an alert that did not go out is not an exception the clock's round has to survive by name, and a subscription the push service retired (`404`/`410`) is dropped rather than insisted on — [Notification](container.md#f-notification-the-pwas-web-push-no-container).
  - **What the container FILLS stays out of the vault's reach, and it is a decision with its price said out loud**: the local CA's private key lives in the clear in the Caddy's `storage` inside the folder, because what writes it is the container and the Main Key does not get inside it — so a copy of the folder carries that key, and with it one issues a certificate that AT and the phone trust. AT does not write and does not seal what the container filled, and the one file it reads there is the public half of the CA. **And that same key in the clear is the declared exception of the backup's gate**: the scan blocks a secret in the clear, and the Caddy's CA **cannot be sealed** — the container reads it open while it stands and rewrites it on certificate renewal —, so the price is this, said here, and the gate exempts it instead of blocking the copy over an object AT cannot seal. **The one thing AT DOES there is prune the certificate of a name it no longer serves**: the set of served names is AT's (`names`), and the key in the clear of a name nobody types serves nothing — the prune belongs to the boot, and the CA's directory is never touched — [The container path](container.md#a-create-the-container-environment-required).
  - **The scan is also the gate over the commit and the push of a git repository, by hooks AT itself writes.** `at-cli secret install-hooks` (`core.hooks`) writes `pre-commit` and `pre-push` into a repository's `.git/hooks/` — the Working Folder's by default, or any other by `--repo`, for the case of a repository that is never a Working Folder (§3, Code and delivery) — and each hook runs `core.scanner.scan()` before letting the commit or the push through: a secret in the clear refuses both, with no flag that turns the gate off. The hook also scans, separately, the Working Folder the committer's own environment configures (`AT_ROOT`), when it is a different one — the product and the instance, always, and never by a fixed path this module remembers.
  - **A folder born before the seal is MIGRATED on the first boot after the swap, and the migration ROTATES.** The values AT draws and that sit in the clear — the gate and the provider's three — are re-drawn and sealed, because a value that was once on disk may have been copied, and sealing the SAME value does not undo a copy: whoever holds the old copy is left with a dead value. It is not decided by swapping the binary: it is an ACT, and `up` asks for the Main Key for it — without the Main Key, no migration. **The price is the rotation's, and it is said**: making the provider's `encryption` another one makes ILLEGIBLE everything it has already encrypted; `session` takes the open sessions down, and `jwt` invalidates the tokens already issued — [The container path](container.md#a-create-the-container-environment-required).
  - Forms stays on until Authelia is proven to work in front of AT; the switch is a set that commits or rolls back — [The switch between paths](paths.md#the-switch-between-paths).
  - A fallen gate closes AT: it says the authentication portal is unavailable, and the way back to Forms mode is the Wizard or the `cli`, both local — [The switch between paths](paths.md#the-switch-between-paths).
  - Every action leaves one line in the `audit` table, and a refusal is one of them — [The audit](#the-audit).
  - What is proved is stored hashed; what AT uses is stored encrypted — [Tables](architecture.md#tables).
  - The persistent state of every service lives in the Working Folder; a service whose state does not fit there does not come in — [General settings](architecture.md#general-settings).
  - **No extension executes code today.** The first class is pure data, and code is the last option — [Extensions](extensions.md).
  - The theme of the authentication portal is not AT's: it stays as neutral as possible, and the `FAQ.md` explains what is underneath — [Interfaces](interfaces.md).
  - Authenticator, WebApp and VPN run in isolated containers on the container path — [Container path](container.md).
  - Execution is without privilege, with elevation only on explicit request — [Privilege](#privilege).
  - Packages and images are pinned in version and hashing — [External dependency](#external-dependency).

### Access and exposure

- AT answers on `127.0.0.1`, and that is the default this product recommends and the address it uses for itself. The address is an adjustment of the installation — the `webapp_host` key of `settings.json`, or `--host` for a single run — and **AT does not refuse to listen outside the loop**. **The reason it does not refuse is that the same address means two different things and the app has no way to tell them apart**: a container's `0.0.0.0` is inside a network, behind the edge and the gate (§7b, §7c), and a machine's `0.0.0.0` is on a LAN. What answers for an address is the network around it, and the environment's security note is where that is evaluated; listening outside the loop is not what this product recommends.

- Exposing demands authentication **and** TLS. Since the TLS is Caddy's, and Caddy is embedded (§3, Code and delivery), exposure on the hosted path is what `at-cli webapp` does by default: the Caddy terminates the TLS and publishes AT, and the WebApp answers on the loop behind it. `--no-caddy` is the way out, back to the loop over HTTP. On the container path, whoever sits at the edge is the Caddy in a container, for the isolation.

- **`--public-port` exists for when something OUTSIDE already terminates the TLS before the embedded Caddy does** — the person's own `tailscale serve`, typically — and that something proxies to a local `--port` that exists only to stay out of its way on the same port. Left unsaid, the two ports are the same one — every test in this repository and every local check, `at-cli webapp --port 12399` included — and nothing moves. Said, it (and never `--port`) is what Authelia's portal carries: the person proves themselves once, and a redirect that sent the browser back to the bind port would send it past whatever actually terminated the TLS — straight at the Caddy's own certificate, which the browser does not trust (TASK-187, found in production: the bind port leaking into a login's `Location:`).

- AT does not deliver HSTS, for an obvious reason: it serves with its own certificate, and HSTS would turn the browser's warning into an error with no way out. That is the price of not depending on a public domain, and of not being able to guarantee trusted TLS.

- **Running from source the WebApp asks nobody in; the `dist`/release binary always asks.** It is the same line that already separates the two worlds in `paths.py`: the `at-cli` from `dist/` owns the folder it sits in, and the checkout's `python -m at` is wherever it was called. What answers is the **artifact** — `sys.frozen`, which the freezer writes and nobody types — and that is why there is no flag and no environment variable for it: both travel, one on a shortcut's command line and one exported in front of a process, and the release would carry the switch that closes its own door. **"Even in debug" is a consequence and not an exception**: there is no execution mode that turns the door off from outside.

- What a run from source waives is the **entrance**, and only it. The folder is still the folder, the tables are still the tables, and `App/settings.json` still holds: a run from the checkout on a folder that has an admin does not start ignoring the admin — it simply asks nobody to prove themselves, and the sentence it prints says so. With no actor at all, the refusal to expose does not apply there either, because it is about an entrance that does not exist.

### The threat model

- AT protects itself from what it can see, and says out loud what it cannot. The first barrier is the path's authenticator: Authelia on the container path, the Forms credential on the hosted one.

- The second barrier is **implicit and is not AT's**: the protection of the phone itself. Allowing a notification to approve an action is trusting that the browser and the push subscription are protected by a locked device. AT **does not audit the phone** — whether it has root, whether it is emulated, whether it has a screen lock — because that is a bank's job and AT is not a bank.

- Keeping the authenticator credential in a password manager or in the browser's vault is the person's choice, and it is dangerous. It is written here instead of hidden.

- The same goes for the outside: a folder backup that does not exist, a machine without a password, an open network. AT measures what it controls and **declares what it does not measure**, instead of giving a grade it cannot sustain — [The security posture](#the-security-posture).

### The audit

- Every action that enters through the core becomes **one line** in the `audit` table: what was changed (`object:action`), where (the table), when (the instant), who (the actor), through which via, the outcome, and the **risk threshold** that was in force when the demand ran. The "why" only exists in a refusal, where it is the reason, stored as an `i18n` key; outside of it, the line does not pretend to have an intention. Cost does not enter — the threshold is not the price of the action but the ruler it was measured against; the demand's risk and the value that raised it stay out.

- **Attempt and refusal are lines**, with the outcome saying what happened — it is the attack-audit view. **A read is not a line**: reading is not doing, and auditing reads would make the trail grow faster than the use.

- The audit is **a log, not a reference**: the ids it cites are raw data, with no `ref`, and that is why it never blocks a `delete` (§4, Tables). It stores no value at all, and **nothing is deleted from it** — deletability is declared in the schema, and `audit` declares that it accepts none.

- Only the admin role carries `audit:read`, and no machine actor has it: an `agente` that could read its own refusals would learn where the wall is and adjust the next attempt.

### The security posture

- The environment has a **security grade**, from `F` to `S`, and it exists for one thing: making the person see the price of what they build and improve their environment. It is not a padlock and it blocks nothing.

- **The grade is per function, and the environment's is the weakest link.** Each graded function — where the Main Key lives, exposure, the second factor of whoever comes in, the scope of the service credentials, what AT itself carries, the plugins, the risk threshold — carries its own grade, and for the environment to be `S` all of them must be `S`. An environment with a bad function cannot call itself good, and the screen always names **which functions are stopping it from rising**.

- **The grade measures what is configured and what you declared, never the truth.** Nobody checks whether you read what a plugin does. That is not a defect: it is the only possible format, and that is why the grade presents itself as "this is what your environment declares", not as "you are safe".

- **Every security impasse is resolved by offering the options and letting the person accept their own security.** The grade informs the price; whoever decides is whoever will live with it. Autonomy is also knowing the security you want for yourself.

- What AT does **not** measure, it says: whether there is no backup of the folder, whether the machine has no password, whether the phone has no lock — AT does not know, and prefers to say so rather than invent a letter.

- **Exposure is graded by REACH and GATE, and not by the path.** What the function measures is who arrives — the reach — and what waits for whoever arrives — the gate —, and the path **has no grade of its own**: hosted and container receive the grade of what is configured in them. The same AT on the local loop is `S` in both, and the same AT behind the Caddy without a gate is `A` in both.

  | level | grade |
  |---|---|
  | local loop (`127.0.0.1`, nothing leaves it) | `S` |
  | private network with a gate (container): the environment's network, Authelia in front, containers isolated (§7b) | `S` |
  | private network with a gate (bundle, no container): the Caddy's TLS, the vendorized Authelia in the same binary, no container isolation | `A` |
  | private network without a gate: off the loop, the Caddy's TLS and Forms, no second factor | `A` |
  | public network: reachable from outside the private network | `F` |

- **A private network counts as local, and it is ONE level.** A private network demands NAT or VPN and is unreachable from outside, and so it counts as a local reach — never as a public exposure. A VPN **does not open a level of its own**: what AT measures is the reach, not the way of getting there.

- **The exposure grade depends on the person's DECLARATION**, because AT **does not measure** NAT, VPN or firewall: what it measures is what is configured and what was declared, and it is the declaration that holds the level up. It is the same rule the other graded functions answer to, and it holds as it holds there: AT measures what it controls and **declares what it does not measure**.

- **Installing does not move the grade.** An OS package, a loose binary with `at-cli install`, a Windows shim: what changes is **how the binary arrives**, not **who reaches it**, and so the exposure letter does not change with the installation.

### Internationalization

- Except for the root door, every document in this repository lives in the **folder of its language**, in the POSIX format (`pt_BR`, `en_US`), aligned with the `i18n` module — it is not BCP 47. The root door is a `README.md` outside the folders, and it has no text beyond the title, the links to the versions in each language, and a short note in `en_US` addressed to LLM agents: that they read in the language that suits their model best, that when in doubt they read in English, and that the English track uses the market's terms and concepts on purpose, so that a name means the same thing here and out there.

- The documents live in `docs/`, **one folder per language**, and each document has its pair in the other language's folder, under the same name. **The name does not repeat the language the folder already says**, and the names are lowercase — only the `README` stays uppercase, for being the name the world already knows.

- **The two languages always change together**, and the approval of a commit covers the pair. `pt_BR` is the source language; where the two diverge, `pt_BR` rules.

- A **working document** lives in the folder of its language and **with no pair**: `docs/pt_BR/backlog.md` is for the agent, changes every day, and translating what changes every day is work nobody reads.

- A **gate in the suite** affirms what that rule promises: the folder of each document, the existence of the pair, the same heading structure in both, and the spelling — both languages go through the repository's checker, and whatever it ignores stays in a versioned list, one per language. Grammar stays with the human reading.

- All code, including comments, object keys, variables, methods and file names, will be in American English (`en_US`). In general, code files are not meant to be read by end users, only audited by developers. So it is more advantageous to keep the base in English for the LLM's processing. This rule holds for what is versioned in the repository; it does not hold for the files AT generates in the Working Folder.

- The whole interface follows `i18n`. No string is saved directly in the code, be it backend or frontend — `cli`, WebApp and Wizard. The source of truth is the `i18n` module, which organizes the texts into large blocks, avoiding a gigantic `pt_BR.json` that the LLM has to read all the time. The blocks are packed into the binary; what stays in the Working Folder is only the active language, in `App/i18n/active`.

- Text that the end user reads is written by humans; the LLM only supports (a draft of the `en_US` translation, review, key checks), as the manifesto says. `pt_BR` is the source and `en_US` the translation, and a human validates both. A key missing in the active language falls back to the source and then to itself, so the gap is visible instead of silent. The key is a **stable id**, never the English sentence: renaming a text must not rename a key. And the suite affirms that both languages carry exactly the same keys, so that a lagging translation is a failing test instead of a surprise.

- On the `cli`, messages and help always come out through `i18n`. The command `at-cli lang pt_BR --include-subcommands` extends the translation to the subcommand names, which is not usual, and that is why it stays optional: the choice is written to `settings.json` (key `subcommands`, holding the language code), the help then shows the language's words (tables, actions and loose words, without accents: `tarefa listar`, `copia fazer`, `idioma`) and the dispatch accepts BOTH spellings, the language's and the canonical `en_US` one (`task list`). Changing the language without the flag puts everything back to the `en_US` spelling; `en_US` is the canonical spelling and has no words of its own. The shell completion stays canonical. And `at-cli lang pt_BR --include-app-folders` answers the question the language change would ask BEFORE it is asked: the person's folders are renamed in the same run, with no terminal to consult — the flag is the person saying yes, and nothing moves without an answer or a typed flag. Language resolution is `$AT_LANG` → `App/i18n/active` → the system locale → `en_US`, and every surface reports where it came from.

### Code and delivery

- AT is written in Python, and the version it demands lives in `pyproject.toml`, at `requires-python`. Its whole code style is based on `PEP 8`. It is delivered as **two artifacts** — `at-cli`, which is the folder and the app, and `at-deploy`, which is §7 — for Windows, Linux and Mac. Its versioning is SemVer, and both carry that number: there is one release, and it publishes both files — on Windows they come out as `at-cli.exe` and `at-deploy.exe`, and on Linux and macOS as `at-cli` and `at-deploy`. Whoever uses the container path needs both; whoever stays on the hosted one needs `at-cli` alone. **The app's is `at-cli` and not `at`**, and the install item below gives the reason.

- `at-cli` carries **Caddy** inside itself. Caddy is a single static binary, and on the hosted path it is the one that terminates TLS and publishes AT; on the container path it runs as a container, for the isolation. There is no release variant: one release only, publishing the two artifacts, and the disk cost is accepted in exchange for that simplicity. Since Caddy goes inside `at-cli`, its version is the artifact's — there is no separate pin for it.

- Updating is replacing the binaries, and **`at-deploy update`** is the act that follows the swap: since `at-deploy` carries the manifest of pinned images, it re-checks the service images and re-pins whatever is out of date, and recreates the containers — the pin only moves forward and never backward. An instance ahead of the binary's pin **warns and proceeds**: it is not downgraded. `at-deploy up` is the first time only — a folder that already has an environment does not come up again through it, and it says the word is `update`. Keeping everything up to date, and without a security failure, is my management, not the binary's.

- **The artifact puts itself where commands are found, and what it creates is a symlink.** `at-cli install` and `at-deploy install` write a link named after the command — `at-cli`, `at-deploy` — pointing at the artifact inside the Working Folder, and the shell's completion beside it. Nothing is fetched and nothing is copied: the binary lives in the folder, updating is replacing the file, and the link follows it — which is why an update asks nothing of the install, and why a link is not state. The directory defaults to `~/.local/bin` and `--bin` chooses another; the choice is recorded in `App/install.json`, so the next install uses it instead of the default and the person does not end up with two links answering to one name. `uninstall` takes the link and the completion back, and never what AT did not put there.

- **The install runs before a Working Folder is confirmed, and creates none.** Every other word is about the folder; this one is about the file and where it sits, so it reads the folder from where the artifact is and links to it. A folder that is not AT's yet has nowhere of AT's to keep the record, and the person is told what that costs instead — `App/` is not created through the back door.

- **The command is `at-cli` because `at` is not a free name.** `at` is a command a UNIX machine may already answer to — the `at`/`atd` job scheduler, one package away on most systems and in the repositories of the distribution this was written on — and the product does not take a name that is not only its own. The brand is AT; the word the machine answers to is one the machine did not already have. The deploy's name was free from the start, which is why only the app's carries the suffix, and it is why the file and the command are the same word on Linux and macOS.

- **The install still SAYS what it finds.** The name above is chosen, not guaranteed: a directory where another command of the link's own name already answers is a real thing, and the install writes the link and says which directory wins, in the order of `PATH`, instead of refusing — installing is an explicit request on the person's own machine, and whoever decides is whoever will live with it (this section, Privilege).

- **The completion is generated from the word tree**, so a word the help offers is a word that completes. On `bash` it lands where `bash-completion` already looks, with no configuration; on `zsh` it lands in the person's own site-functions directory, which is not on the default `fpath` — and AT prints the line that puts it there rather than editing a shell configuration file that is not its own. A shell AT does not know is a sentence, and nothing is written for it.

- **On Windows the install does not exist yet**, and it is the one platform where this rule does not hold: there a symlink is not a symlink, so "nothing is copied" would be false. It waits, named in the backlog — the same way the backup waits.

- Every update that changes structure brings its **migration**, and it runs on the first start after the binary is replaced. The folder knows how far it has migrated, and that stamp lives in `App/` — along with the rest of what AT writes — never inside the published declaration. The declaration remains pure data, which you edit; what the two authors sign stays separate.

- A migration **handles its own impact and decides what to do with it**. Naming a folder moves a path, and what a person points at from outside — a vault of another tool, a copy script — is what breaks later, when that other thing is opened. What touches the person's own folders therefore asks before it moves and says what breaks; what touches only AT's own folder does not ask, because there is nothing outside to break.

- The migration **never overwrites what already exists**: it adds what is missing and reports what it skipped. In a collision, your edit rules, and AT warns instead of choosing for you — the same choice as the image pin. And a published migration **is not edited**: folders have already run it, and a fix becomes the next migration.

- Comments in the code explain only what the code does not say by itself: why that piece is necessary and, when it is not obvious, what it does. For history, we use `git`. There is no need to note dates of decisions. See, the code must speak for itself (as [chess speaks for itself](https://pt.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controv%C3%A9rsia_Magnus_Carlsen%E2%80%93Hans_Niemann)). If we need kilos of comments, maybe it is not so well written after all.

- **The folders a person opens answer in the person's language, and they are capitalized.** `Tabelas/`, `Biblioteca/`, `Projetos/`, `App/`, `Compartilhada/`, `Harness/` and `Cofre/` in `pt_BR`; `Tables/`, `Library/`, `Projects/`, `App/`, `Shared/`, `Harness/` and `Vault/` in `en_US`. The name on disk is the **face**; the machine reads the **internal name** — `data`, `prose`, `projects`, `app`, `shared`, `harness`, `strongbox` —, declared in one place and resolved through it: a localized name is a declaration, never an `if` in the code that acts, and the declaration a table publishes cites the internal name, never the face. The spellings `Arquivos/` and `Files/` are `prose/`'s **retired** ones: the resolution still accepts them, which is why a folder that did not migrate — or that declined the rename — does not disappear. Two folders are the exception, and both are deliberate: `App/` does not follow the language, because the language is recorded inside it, and `Harness/` does not, because `pt_BR` took the word whole — both languages spell it the same way.

- **The functions of AT live in a table, `function`.** Each row binds an internal function — `library`, `strongbox`, `code` and `harness` — to the folders it works in and says whether it is enabled. The `library` accepts several folders; the others, one. A folder is a path relative to the Working Folder or an absolute one, inside or outside the repository. The table is born seeded with today's folders (`Library/`, `Vault/`, `Harness/`; `code` has none until the person chooses), so an existing instance stays the same. `projects`, `shared`, `app`, `data` and `convert` are fixed base structure and stay outside the map; `Files/` is the old name of the Library, not a function. Disabling a function keeps its folders and data. For now the table is the map; the core's resolution still reads the names in `paths.py`.

- **Changing the language renames the folders, and the rename is offered and never automatic.** AT says every implication before doing it — what breaks outside the folder first of all, because a vault another tool points at is the thing the person does not see break until later. Whoever declines keeps the name they had: AT reads every spelling the product has given a folder, and says what that costs.

- The binary lives in the folder and that folder belongs to the binary. It is the Working Folder. If you open the binary inside the `Downloads` folder, the `Downloads` folder is its own. On the first load you will be warned that: "AT was opened in the folder <folder>. Since there is no `App/settings.json` file here, this is understood to be a new Working Folder. Do you confirm that AT may use this folder? Using an empty folder, anywhere on your computer, is recommended. Cancel and move the file to another folder if you disagree.". Or something of the sort.

- The backup of the Working Folder is AT's promise: **the copied folder is enough for everything to work again**, with no binary swap in between. That is why nothing regenerates on its own — neither what was extracted, nor what was downloaded — and the only exception is what is platform-specific: a folder that changes operating system re-extracts what no longer serves. Everything AT writes lives in `App/`, in a visible folder precisely so that nobody mistakes it for junk: adjustments, active language, counters, migration stamp, plugin state, what was extracted to run, cache and the state of the services.

- AT **has no watchdog and does not propose to have one**. It is aware that everything can be modified from outside, and if the person knows how to modify it, let them: what it does is measure what it finds and say what that costs, never police the folder.

- With no terminal to ask, there is no first run: AT exits with code `1` instead of assuming a folder. And declining creates nothing — AT says where to move the binary and exits without an error, because declining is not an error.

### Privilege

- AT, by default, is architected to run without administration permissions (i.e. it does not use `root` or `Administrator`). However, building the environment of §7 inevitably demands elevation at some point. In those cases privilege elevation will happen through an explicit request, with the reason on display.

- The closed list of what demands elevation is not written before the function: it is born with the implementation that asks for it. Each item that enters it is a security decision and passes through this section.

- **The list, so far: the low-port rule, and only it — and `at-deploy` is the one that asks.** §7a's edge publishes on `443` — on the loop, or on the private network's address when there is one (§7e) — , and a machine that ships the kernel's `net.ipv4.ip_unprivileged_port_start` — every Linux does — refuses that bind to a user process. `at-deploy` asks, with the reason and the exact command on display, and what it asks for is the NARROWEST thing that works: the floor moves to the lowest port the environment publishes, never to zero. It lasts until the next boot, and the next boot asks again — an explicit request made again, not a permission remembered behind the person's back.

- **`at-cli` cannot ask, and that is read off the artifact.** The environment of §7 left `at-cli` whole and became `at-deploy` (item 27): the elevation MOVED with it and is not duplicated, so what this section promises about running without administration permissions is a property of what the file IS and not of how it behaves. The two artifacts share the folder's STATE and not an API, and the deploy tool is the one that writes it.

### Portability

- Data is always POSIX. `LF` or `/`. Conversions to Windows only for display (as in the Working Folder welcome message, above) or for the `syscalls`.

### External dependency

- Dependencies on packages and images are pinned in version and hashing — be it packages, be it external images.

- Caddy and `gitleaks` are the declared exceptions, and not by carelessness: both travel embedded in the binary, so whoever pins them is the binary itself, with AT's SemVer.

- **Vendorized means pinned by manifest and pulled at build, not versioned.** The third-party binaries (Caddy, `gitleaks`, Authelia and the checker's tarball) do not live in the repository: `at/vendor/*/manifest.json` holds, versioned, the name, the version, the official URL and the `sha256` of each file, and `python3 -m release.fetch` downloads each one into the same place and the same name as always, checking the hash. A file that is already there and matches the pin is not downloaded again; one that does not match is deleted and never used. `release.build` calls the fetch before packing and fails, naming the missing file, if any did not arrive; the artifact still embeds all of them, as before. A clone has to run `python3 -m release.fetch` before running or packing, and the tests that read the real bytes skip when they are not there.

- **What may travel inside the artifact is a question of license, and the license travels with it.** A third party only enters the binary if its license permits, and whatever the license demands comes with it — the copyright notice and the text. Since the artifact is ONE file and not a folder, the notice does not sit beside it: it sits inside, and comes out through each artifact's `license` word (`at-cli license`, `at-deploy license`), which says what THAT artifact carries and never what the other one carries. The product's license is `MIT` (`LICENSE.md`), and it is that license which permits embedding this way; a third party whose license does not permit it stays out, and the decision to embed is taken with the disk cost said out loud.

- **The declaration is one, and each text is the vendor's own file.** `at/vendor/licenses/manifest.json` says, per component, the name, the version, the `SPDX` identifier, which artifacts carry it and where the text lives; nothing is declared by hand in two places, and it is from it that the `SBOM` (item 46) generates the license lines. What travels is the vendor's file, never a paraphrase — the `LICENSE` inside the Caddy's tarball and the one inside the `gitleaks`' are the same ones the project keeps beside them —, and the release promises those notices: a build that loses any of them is refused.

- **The machine the release is built on is a dependency too, and it is pinned like one.** A frozen binary carries the machine that built it: the interpreter's extension modules travel with the artifact, and each one records the version of the library it was linked against — so the release's glibc floor is a property of WHERE it was built and not of the code. The release declares that floor, is built in the base the declaration pins, and is refused when the artifact needs more than it promised.

- **Nothing experimental comes in**, not even as a development tool: stable components only. Alpha, beta, release candidate and preview stay out, and whatever the vendor marks as experimental stays out with them — the promise that AT does not change under your feet cannot rest on a surface whose owner says it will change.

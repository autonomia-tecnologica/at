# 4. Architecture [normative]

### Layer separation

- AT is structured in layers. `core` handles every rule of the application. It is not a monolithic block: it is broken into modules and files according to the subject. But everything passes through it. No layer outside `core` resolves a path of its own.

- `ui` is a form of layer, but it has more. AT is born with three surfaces: `cli`, WebApp and Wizard. All of them are only different ways of interacting with `core`. Not everything done by the `cli` is available in the WebApp. But everything in the `WebApp + Wizard` is available in the cli.

- Another very important layer is `data`. This layer is responsible for accessing the tables. Structured data saved as `.jsonl` inside the `./data` folder. The `./data` folder is created automatically on the first run, with the system tables in force.

- AT also supports managing miscellaneous files. It is the `prose` layer. Any file that is not structured data is this layer's business. The `./prose` folder, also created on the first run, comes with a welcome file generated by `i18n` in the active language chosen on the first run (`Bem-vindo.md` in `pt_BR`, `Welcome.md` in `en_US`), to make the next steps easier.

- External services are handled by the `services` layer. This is so that the layer is as tool-agnostic as possible: the **service** has a canonical name and the **tool** that fulfils it is replaceable. It is in this layer that the services part of the product lives — every configured service lives here, with its state in the folder.

- Secrets are managed by the `encryption` layer.

- Natural language interpretation is the `nlu` layer: it turns a sentence into an action **proposal**, and nothing else. It does not write, does not decide and has no ceiling of its own — whoever validates and writes is `core`.

- The security posture is the `posture` layer: it measures what is configured, adds up the per-function grades by the weakest link and answers the screen and the API. It measures; it does not block.

### Tables

- AT has an engine of its own for managing structured data. The term Table is used here, or `table` internally. The identity of the table is an id, and the id is singular (`task`, `project`, `note`, `reminder`, `secret`, `actor`, `role`, `audit`, `job`): it is what names the table everywhere, including in the command (`at-cli task list`) and in the schema. The file of rows is the one that goes in the plural. So `data/tasks.jsonl` is the table itself and `data/schema/task.json` is its schema.

- The tables `task`, `project`, `note`, `reminder`, `secret`, `actor`, `role`, `audit` and `job` are system defaults. They serve to deliver an inherent functionality. System tables are not deletable, and **the deletability of a row is declared in the table's schema**: `audit` declares that no row of its own is erased.

- The `job` table holds the **demand that could not run**, and the act the **clock** drives: the act it will perform, the arguments that act needs, the actor that owns it, the instant it is about and the state of its life — `waiting`, `approved`, `refused`, `done` and `scheduled`. What waits is a **job, and not a queue**: the demand that reaches the threshold becomes a row of this table, in the state that says it waits, and the answer names which. Who waits is about **presence, and not about type**: the act a person types or clicks never waits, because the act IS the approval, and what runs on its own waits at the threshold — that is why the `agente`'s demand waits and the person's does not. The job runs with the via `engine` and with the actor that owns it, so its ceiling is that actor's. The approval is a **line of its own in the audit**, with the actor and the via it came through, separate from the job that waited — and no machine actor carries `job:approve` or `job:refuse`, for the same reason §3 gives for `audit:read`.

- **The `when` is the instant the job is about, and what it says depends on who drives it.** The demand that waited carries the instant it **arrived** and waits for a person; the job the **clock** drives carries the **next instant at which it must round**, and `scheduled` is the state that says so — the fifth, and the only one a job that repeats ever rests in. The clock **reads** the `when` and **rewrites** it: after every round the instant moves on by the cadence, which is one number for the whole folder and has the **minute** as its granularity — a job's declaration says WHEN it starts, and the rhythm after that belongs to the clock, so whatever needs an interval of its own reads it the way the versioning write does. **The clock lives in the WebApp's process while it is up**, and it lives there because today what manages a job IS the WebApp: it leaves when the engine leaves. A round runs with the via `engine` and with the actor that owns the job, it faces the threshold — what runs on its own waits —, and a round that ends waiting for approval **waits as a job**: the same row, the same state, with no second model, no queue and no lock. A round that passed while AT was down **runs on the next start, once and only once** — the next instant is measured from the round that happened and not from the appointment that was missed —, and leaves its line saying it was recovered.

- User tables allow full CRUD of the table itself.

- The table schema has many features, such as default fields in the display and default order in the display of tables. The display labels it carries are `i18n` keys, never the final text. The id counter does not live in the schema: it counts what happened in this folder, and the schema is published — so it lives in `App/counters.json`, and it is written before the row, because an id handed out is spent even if the write that followed failed.

- The schema declares **field types**, and each type has a fixed rule, with no choice per field:
  - **hashed** — stores the result of a secure derivation, made with `libsodium`; the value does not come back on a read, it is only compared.
  - **sealed** — carries a value AT **uses**, sealed with the key of its own service or purpose; it does not come back on a read, and it is the key that opens it and not the engine.
  - **instant** — a point in time: ISO, always UTC, always with milliseconds (`2026-09-22T13:04:07.123Z`). Locality is detected on display, never stored.
  - **day** — a calendar day, with no time and no timezone, resolved by the locality of whoever writes. "Today" is your today.
  - The common types — text, number, date, reference, enumeration — as they always were.

- **What is proved is stored hashed; what AT uses is stored encrypted.** `secret` is of the second kind, `actor` is of the first.

- **Whoever acts is an actor**, and every actor is a row of the `actor` table, with the field `type` holding `person`, `engine` or `agent`. The row carries the identity, the role and the credential in the hashed field. The engine acts in what belongs to the engine; the `agent` is whoever uses AT without being a person, and it is the one with a ceiling of its own — whoever types a sentence is the person, and the via is `nlu`.

- The permission catalogue is declared in the code, and it is not loose data: the permission has the form `object:action`, the objects and the actions are a closed list, and the display labels come from `i18n` like everything else. The objects go beyond the tables: the settings, the services, the plugins and the interpreter's vocabulary are also objects, with permissions of their own. A role is a row of the `role` table with a set of permissions, and the `actor` table points at the role. A role naming a permission that does not exist does not bring AT down: it warns, and the warning is what gets read.

### The Vault

- **The Vault is where everything AT seals lives.** To perform certain actions, such as accessing external service APIs, AT needs access to secrets. Every secret AT **stores** is written into the `secret` table (`data/secrets.jsonl`) — a secret that is only compared, such as an actor's credential, lives hashed in its own row and not in the Vault (§4, Tables: **what is proved stays hashed, what AT uses stays in the Vault, encrypted**). There are the metadata and the secret itself, saved in encrypted form.

- AT's encryption starts from a **Main Key**, which is what the person knows and types: at least 16 characters, and it is a phrase. "Mariazinha comeu todo o pudim", in lowercase and without spaces, is safer than `Mariazinha@123`. From it the **derived keys** derive, one per service or purpose. The Main Key recovers any lost derived key.

- **There is no second cryptography system in AT.** Every cipher the product holds — the Vault, the gate, whatever each service signs or encrypts for itself — goes through this one door: the Main Key and what derives from it. A path that ciphered outside it, or that kept a ready-made key instead of deriving it from the phrase when one is missing, would be a cryptography of its own that AT does not have — even under another name, even if it only existed on one of the boot paths.

- A derived key is scoped by a **name** — the canonical name of a service, or a purpose written as one — and the Vault keeps no catalogue of names: the services are §7's and the plugins', and a list here would be the Vault naming a world it does not own. What it refuses is a scope that is not a name, and what it holds is that two names a character apart are two keys.

- It is recommended to keep the Main Key outside the machine, in a safe place. Printing it and storing it among personal documents that are hard to reach, for example.

- In cryptography there is no magic. Either you type the Main Key every time AT needs a secret, or you type it once and it holds while AT is up, or AT stores what it needs to unlock itself. **The choice is graded, and the security grade says the price of each:** typing when AT starts is `S`; keeping it unlocked in the system user's folder (`~/.at/` on Unix, `%APPDATA%` on Windows) is `A`; keeping it inside the Working Folder is `B` — and the last one is allowed, because then the folder backup carries everything.

- AT runs on its own when it needs to: a scheduled job does not ask for a password on every execution, and its definition and its state live in the `job` table (§4, Tables). What it does not do is choose for you where the key lives.

- The Main Key is the first admin's credential: the same string proves whoever comes in and unlocks the Vault. What is compared is the derivation — its hash, kept in the actor's row — and not the key: that is why entering does not require unlocking the Vault, and why what is proved lives hashed in the row and not in `secret`.

### General settings

- AT's settings live inside `App/settings.json`, inside the working folder. There live properties in key-value format. `i18n` does not live here: the translation blocks are packed into the binary and the active language lives in `App/i18n/active`.

- **The settings are reachable through the core API** (`setting:read` and `setting:edit` are permissions), because that is where everything that acts passes through — including the reading of the **risk threshold** that was in force at the instant a demand ran, which is what the audit line records. There is one door: `list` and `view` are the read and `edit` is the write, the `cli` reaches it by `setting list|view|edit` (§5: the `cli` reaches everything), and what AT writes for itself — the shape version — the door refuses.

- No secret lives in `settings.json`. It holds permission and adjustment — such as the permission for an actor to come in through Basic, or the risk threshold — never the credential itself.

- Each service may have persisted data. In that case, it lives in `App/services/<service>`, such as `App/services/vpn` or `App/services/spelling-checker`. The service name is canonical and does not change with the tool that fulfils it: `spelling-checker` is the service, LanguageTool is who fulfils it today (§7d) — as a process AT itself brings up and takes down, not as a container — and whatever replaces it one day renames neither folders nor keys. What is a display label is an `i18n` key.

- That folder is not a convenience, it is a condition of entry: a service only enters §7 if its persistent state fits in there. A service that keeps its life in some volume nobody copies makes the folder backup lie.
